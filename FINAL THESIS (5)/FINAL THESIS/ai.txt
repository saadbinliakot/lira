Abstract
Modern astronomical observatories produce high-cadence, multi-wavelength data
capturing the variability of black hole accretion systems, which transition between
distinct physical states di!ering in spectral emission, accretion geometry, and jet
activity. Observational datasets are, however, unlabelled: the accretion state responsible for each observation is not directly observable. Existing causal discovery
frameworks require predefined environment labels, surrogate domain indices, or prior
knowledge of the latent state structure. Methods that do discover regimes jointly
with causal structure, notably CASTOR, rely on expectation-maximisation over the
full series and provide no explicit statistical test for whether a proposed boundary
reflects a genuine mechanism change rather than finite-sample variation. Furthermore, the temporal dependence present in high-cadence monitoring data violates
the i.i.d. assumptions underlying many causal discovery algorithms, inflating test
statistics and producing spurious boundaries.
We propose LIRA (Latent Invariant Regime Analysis), a causal artificial intelligence framework for discovering hidden causal regimes and regime-specific causal
structures directly from unlabelled time-series observations. The method is motivated by the power-law physics of the Fundamental Plane of Black Hole Activity,
which justifies modelling observational variables within a log-linear structural causal
framework. Regime discovery proceeds by recursive binary partitioning guided
by a Chow-test invariance criterion, with temporal autocorrelation accommodated
through a first-order autoregressive extension yielding a lagged Chow F -statistic.
Candidate partitions are gated by the Bayesian Information Criterion, enabling automatic determination of the number of latent regimes without prior specification.
Once stable regimes are identified, regime-specific causal graphs are recovered using
DYNOTEARS, and the resulting causal e!ects are subjected to a formal validation
battery comprising refutation tests, sensitivity analysis to unobserved confounding,
and graph falsification.
On synthetic accretion data constructed so that the two regimes are distinguishable
only by causal mechanism, with all variable means equalised across regimes, LIRA
recovers the true regime boundary with an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.998, while
structure-blind clustering baselines achieve ARI → 0.012. Structural coe”cients
are recovered to within 0.02 of their generating values in both regimes, including
the correct identification of the causally isolated variable in each. A single pooled
model fitted without segmentation recovers a spurious averaged structure matching
neither regime. All estimated causal e!ects survive a refutation battery, and sensitivity analysis establishes that e!ect robustness to unobserved confounding scales
with e!ect magnitude (r = 0.89), implying that the soft state’s weaker radio–X-ray
coupling warrants correspondingly weaker causal claims.

Keywords: Causal Artificial Intelligence, Causal Inference, Latent Regime Discovery, Black Hole Accretion, Structural Break Detection, Causal Discovery, DYNOTEARS,
Structural Causal Models, Time Series Analysis, Observational Astronomy

Chapter 1
Introduction
This section introduces the research context, motivation, and objectives of the study.
It presents scientific challenges in the search for hidden causal regimes in observational data from black hole accretion and sets the agenda for a causal framework for
artificial intelligence that can meet those challenges. The chapter further presents
the proposed research objectives, the method used to conduct the research, and the
scope and limitations of the research.

1.1

Background

For the past two decades observational astronomy has advanced greatly, both in the
quantity and quality of the astrophysical data. The high-cadence, multi-wavelength
surveys from missions like Chandra, XMM-Newton, and NICER [7], [9], [18] have
turned our view of the universe from a series of static snapshots into a data-rich
cinema. Challenge our traditional paradigms for data interpretation to be able
to decipher the complexity of the signals we get. Black hole accretion systems are
among the most studied astrophysical systems and provide interesting environments
to probe high energy physical processes. Black holes are regions of spacetime warped
so extremely that the speed needed to escape it has to be greater than the speed
of light. We know that speed of light in vacuum is the fastest and is the universal
speed limit. Nothing with mass can escape the event horizon and can travel at
or faster than this, because it would require infinite energy. However, the regions
surrounding these voids are far from silent. Accretion - the process of the gravitational capture and inward spiralling of matter - converts gravitational potential
energy into radiation with unparallel e”ciency [2], [8]. It is one of the most pervasive and important phenomena in the Universe. Rather than a steady glow, black
hole binaries and Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) shows profound variability across
multiple timescales [13], [15]. This variability is not random noise. It manifests as
distinct ”accretion states” - regimes where the geometry of the flow and the mechanism of energy release undergo fundamental shifts. Active black hole accretion disk
transitions occurs primarily across two primary observational regimes - Hard State
and Soft State [11]. Understanding the transitions between these states is critical
to decipher the life cycle of a black hole. Despite the obvious complexity of these
state transitions, a state of order emerges in the form of power-law scaling relations.
Whether observing a stellar mass black hole at our own galaxy or a supermassive
black hole at the centre of a distant quasar, certain physical properties appear to

1.2

Rationale of the Study

Black hole accretion systems exhibit profound variability across multiple timescales,
transitioning between physically distinct states. The mechanisms governing jet formation, spectral emission, and energy dissipation di!er fundamentally. Even after
this, observational datasets arrive unlabelled - the physical state of each observation
must be inferred rather than read directly. Current practice relies on manual classification by domain experts, a process that is time-intensive. It also does not scale
to the data volumes produced by modern high-cadence instruments such as NICER.
Beyond the labelling problem, existing causal analyses of accretion systems are limited by their inability to account for regime-dependent changes in causal structure.
If we fit a single pooled causal model across observations drawn from physically
distinct accretion states, it will produce biased coe”cient estimates and obscure the
true coupling laws governing jet-disk interaction. If the causal relationship between
X-ray luminosity and radio emission obeys one functional form in the hard state
and a di!erent form in the soft state, this di!erence cannot be recovered without
first identifying which observations belong to which state. The development of an
automated, objective framework for latent causal regime discovery would therefore
carry direct scientific value. It would enable the systematic causal characterization
of newly obtained observational datasets without manual intervention, and could
reveal regime-dependent physical coupling laws that correlational studies are structurally unable to detect. This represents a meaningful contribution to the growing
application of causal artificial intelligence to observational science.

Problem Statement

This thesis addresses the absence of methods capable of discovering latent causal
regimes from unlabelled observations without requiring pre-specified environment
labels, surrogate domain indices, or prior knowledge of the number of hidden states.
Existing causal discovery methods rely on requirements unmet by observational astrophysical data. Specifically, ICP (Peters et al., 2016) [20] and IRM (Arjovsky et
al., 2019) [25] require environment labels as a prerequisite. CD-NOD (Huang et al.,
2020) [29] requires a surrogate variable encoding the changing conditions—typically
a time or domain index—which is unavailable when the latent state is itself unknown. Furthermore, CCSL (Chen et al., 2021) requires multiple independent
subject datasets, a structure incompatible with the single continuous observational
records typical of X-ray binary monitoring.
Additionally, traditional clustering methods group observations by feature similarity rather than causal mechanism stability. They cannot detect regime boundaries where observable distributions overlap while the underlying causal laws di!er.
Compounding these limitations, high-cadence accretion data is temporally autocorrelated. Standard causal discovery algorithms assume independent and identically
distributed (i.i.d.) observations; when this assumption is violated, the underlying
statistical tests produce inflated statistics and spurious regime boundary detections.
Ultimately, no existing framework addresses this combination of unlabelled data, an
unknown regime count, and temporal dependence simultaneously using invariancebased recursive partitioning.

1.4

Objectives

The general objective of this study is to develop and validate a framework capable
of discovering hidden causal regimes and their associated causal structures directly
from unlabelled, temporally-dependent observational data without requiring environment labels, a surrogate nonstationarity index, or prior knowledge of the number
of latent states, and finally apply this framework to black hole accretion observations. The specific objectives are:
1. To formulate accretion observables within a structural causal framework grounded
in established scaling physics, so that the model’s assumptions are physically

Methodology in Brief

The proposed methodology operates as a two-stage pipeline. The input are a
set of unlabelled multi-wavelength accretion observations. All variables are logtransformed, motivated by the power-law structure of black hole accretion physics
which renders multiplicative relationships linear in log space and justifies the use
of ordinary least squares regression. Stage 1 performs latent regime discovery. A
global OLS model is fitted to all data, and a Chow F-test [1] assesses whether a single causal mechanism is su”cient. For time-series data, a first-order autoregressive
lag term is added to the design matrix to absorb temporal autocorrelation, yielding a lagged Chow F-statistic with q = 6 parameters per model. If invariance is
rejected, a systematic search over all candidate splitting variables and thresholds selects the split maximizing the F-statistic. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
[3] then determines whether the split is accepted or the node is declared as a leaf.
This process recurses until all partitions satisfy the BIC stopping criterion, with the
number of regimes K determined automatically. State 2 recovers a directed causal
graph within each stable partition using the DYNOTEARS algorithm, a dynamic
extension of the NOTEARS algorithm explicitly designed for time-series data. The
framework is validated on synthetic data generated from structural causal models
with known graph structures, allowing ground-truth evaluation via the Adjusted
Rand Index [4]. Real observational data from the WATCHDOG catalogue [21] and
the Merloni et al. (2003) [10] Fundamental Plane compilation are soon to be tested.

Multivariate Time-Series and Non-Stationarity

i. Time Series Data
Time series data are a sequence of observations collected at di!erent points in time,
where each observation is associated with a specific timestamp. The order of the
data, or temporal ordering, is meaningful, and changing this order changes the
structure and meaning of the data. More formally, a multivariate time series dataset
is denoted as X = {x1 , x2 , x3 , . . . , xn }, consisting of n distinct variables where each
variable is sequentially measured across T timesteps [36]. In time series data, a
specific variable at a given timestep t may be influenced by any other variable in
the same timestep, or by variables from previous timesteps ranging from 0 to t ↑ 1.
The latter is known as lagged e!ects. Theoretically, a lagged e!ect can propagate
from an infinite earlier point in time. However, assuming an infinite window is
not feasible. In a practical sense for Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) learning, this
window is restricted to a designated maximum time lag, represented as Imax .
ii. Stationary and Non-Stationary Data
In stationary data, the statistical properties of the data like mean and variance
remain constant along every value of T . The data may fluctuate here and there,
but the statistical properties never truly shift along any time. On the other hand,
in non-stationary data, the statistical properties may shift systematically or totally
unpredictably at any time.
iii. Temporal Regime
In time series analysis and causal discovery, a temporal regime is defined by a consecutive window of time in which the data’s statistical properties, rules, or causal
structure remains constant. For example, let’s assume for a time-series dataset,
timestep t = 1 to t = m has the same properties. So, t = 1 to m belong in the same
regime. But the properties shift from t = m + 1 and stay constant until t = k. So,
t = m + 1 to k belongs to another regime.
iv. Strict Stationary Assumption
Many traditional time series and causal discovery models heavily rely on a strict
stationary assumption, meaning they assume that the underlying rules and patterns
of the data remain constant along every timestep. In this assumption, the time
index is considered to be independent of the data distribution, which implies that
the causal dependencies remain unchanged over the whole period of time. However,
in dynamic real-world systems, this assumption fails because most real-life data is
non-stationary and its underlying distribution shifts following a pattern or randomly
to a di!erent temporal regime. Traditional models cannot address this problem and
may fail to produce accurate discoveries because they cannot address the changing
regimes or multi-regime properties.

i. Black Holes
Black holes are among the most mysterious and most studied phenomena in the
Universe. Much progress has been made in our knowledge and understanding of
these systems, but many aspects of their behaviour are the subject of intensive
investigation and are not yet fully understood.
A black hole is a region of space-time that is so dense that nothing, not even light,
can escape once it crosses a boundary called the event horizon. Black holes are not
directly observable via emitted light but are inferred through their gravitational and
electromagnetic e!ects on nearby matter. Black holes can form when a massive star
dies. Einstein’s General Theory of Relativity tells us that mass and energy warp the
fabric of space-time. The most extreme examples of this curvature are black holes.
The event horizon is the boundary beyond which information cannot return to an
external observer.

ii. Accretion and Accretion Disks
Accretion is the primary mechanism by which black holes grow and become observable. It is the process of gravitational capture and the inward spiralling of matter
toward a central massive body. In the vacuum of space, most matter has some degree of spin or angular momentum. Gas and dust do not fall directly into a black
hole in a straight line. Instead, they swirl around it. During this process, the gravitational potential energy of the falling matter is converted into kinetic energy and
heat. This conversion is highly e”cient, with accretion onto black holes representing
one of the most e”cient known mechanisms of energy release [2], [8]. As a result,
the surrounding regions can become among the most luminous astrophysical sources
in the Universe.
As matter approaches a black hole, it does not fall straight in, but instead forms a
large, thin disk, spiralling around the black hole. This disk is called an accretion disk.
Inside the disk, internal friction (viscosity) causes the gas to lose its orbital energy
and slowly spiral inwards. The friction generates enormous heat, with temperatures
in the millions of degrees.
The disk’s temperature is not uniform; the outer regions are relatively cooler, while
the innermost regions reach such extreme temperatures that they glow brightly in
the X-ray part of the electromagnetic spectrum. This emission allows astronomers
to map the geometry and behaviour of the disk even though the black hole itself
remains dark.

iii. Black Hole X-ray Binaries
Black holes are generally divided into three categories according to their mass:
stellar-mass, supermassive, and intermediate-mass. The mass ranges that define
each group are approximate. In our own galaxy, we often find stellar-mass black
holes (typically 5 to 30 times the mass of our Sun) in binary systems. These are
known as Black Hole X-ray Binaries (BHXBs).
In these systems, the black hole orbits a normal companion star. The black hole’s
gravity is strong enough to strip gas from the outer layers of the companion star.
This captured gas feeds the accretion disk, creating a laboratory where we observe
rapid changes in the black hole’s feeding habits over timescales of weeks or months.
These are known as Black Hole X-ray Binaries (BHXBs) [13], [15].
iv. Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)
On a much larger scale, Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) represent supermassive black
holes at the centres of distant galaxies. These black holes are millions or even billions
of times more massive than our Sun.
Despite the vast di!erence in size, the underlying physics of how they consume
matter remains the same as their smaller stellar-mass cousins. Because they are
so large, the changes in their behaviour happen much more slowly. These changes
take thousands or millions of years, which is why we use smaller X-ray binaries as
fast-forward models to understand the life cycles of these galactic giants.
v. Accretion States
Black holes do not consume matter at a steady or unchanging rate. Instead, they
transition between distinct accretion states - physical regimes where the geometry
of the gas flow and the mechanism of energy release change fundamentally. These
states are identified by the spectrum of the light they emit.
vi. Hard State
In the Hard State, the black hole is typically at a lower accretion rate. The accretion
disk is often truncated, meaning the bright, inner part of the disk is replaced by a
hot, di!use cloud of gas called a corona.
A defining feature of this state is the presence of a steady radio jet (a powerful
beam of particles shot out from the centre at nearly the speed of light). The light
we observe is dominated by high-energy (or Hard ) X-rays, which flicker rapidly.
vii. Soft State
When the black hole begins to consume matter more rapidly, it transitions into the
Soft State. In this regime, the accretion disk moves all the way inward to the closest
possible stable orbit.
In the Soft State, the radio jet is quenched or disappears entirely. The emission is
dominated by the steady heat of the disk itself, appearing as a Soft X-ray glow.
This state is disk-dominated, whereas the Hard State is jet-dominated.

Fundamentals of Causal Inference

To understand the mechanisms governing each temporal regime, we rely on the formal framework of causal inference. This section introduces the necessary graphical
and structural definitions, adopting the framework detailed by Neal (2020).
i. Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs)
A graph is a mathematical structure consisting of a set of nodes (or vertices) and a
set of edges, where each edge represents a relationship between two nodes. In the
context of causal inference, nodes represent the variables or features of the dataset
[32].
In a directed graph, edges contain arrows indicating the direction of the relationship.
The node from which an edge originates is called the parent node, and the node the
edge points to is called the child node.
A path is a sequence of nodes connected by edges, regardless of the direction of the
arrows. A directed path, however, is a sequence of nodes connected by directed edges
that all point in the same forward direction.
For a given node X, its descendants, denoted as de(X), consist of all nodes Y for
which there exists a directed path from X to Y (this includes children, children of
children, and so forth). Conversely, the ancestors of Y , denoted as an(Y ), consist of
all nodes X for which there exists a directed path from X to Y (including parents,
parents of parents, etc.).

A Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) is a directed graph that contains no cycles;
namely, there does not exist any directed path that starts and ends at the same
node. Consequently, no node can be an ancestor or descendant of itself [32].
ii. Causal Graphs
A variable X is considered a cause of a variable Y if an intervention or deliberate
change in X results in a change in the distribution of Y ; this underlying mechanism
is referred to as causation [32].
Within the framework of a directed graph, the Causal Edges Assumption states that
every parent node is a direct cause of all its child nodes.
Consequently, a Causal Graph is formally defined as a Directed Acyclic Graph
(DAG) that strictly satisfies the Causal Edges Assumption. In such a graph, every
directed path from one node to another implies a flow of causation.
iii. Statistical Independence and Dependence

vi. The Discovery Postulates: Markov and Faithfulness
Every mathematical field stands on axioms and postulates. Similarly, to successfully
discover a causal graph G from the probability distribution P of observational data,
it is necessary to establish a mathematical bridge between the SCM and statistical independence. This bridge relies on two foundational postulates: the Causal

independent of all its non-descendants, given its direct parents. Formally,
for any node X in a DAG G, where PA(X) represents its parents and

X ↔↔ ND(X) | PA(X)

This assumption guarantees that the causal graph naturally imposes the
correct conditional independencies on the joint probability distribution.
If two variables are d-separated in the graph G, they must be statistically
independent in the data P .
2. The Faithfulness Assumption
The Faithfulness Assumption is the logical converse of the Markov Assumption. A probability distribution P is considered faithful to a DAG
G if every conditional independence observed in the data is strictly the
result of a structural d-separation in the graph. Formally, if X ↔↔ Y | Z

and coincidentally cancel each other out to create a fake statistical independence.
Together, these postulates guarantee that the structural d-separations in the causal
graph perfectly map to the statistical conditional independencies in the observable
time-series data, making the unsupervised discovery of the DAG mathematically
possible.

Unsupervised Learning and Clustering

Because the structural boundaries of real-world temporal regimes are rarely known
in advance, discovering these shifts requires analyzing the data without predefined
labels. This section discusses the application of clustering and temporal segmentation
to identify these hidden causal boundaries.
i. The Need for Unsupervised Learning
We have theoretically established the basic theoretical framework for modeling
causal structure. In this section, we address a major methodological challenge of
causal modeling, which is detecting the temporal regimes without knowing the structural boundaries of the data. We frame this as an unsupervised learning problem.
In traditional supervised learning algorithms, models are trained with data with
input features x where the actual outputs y are known beforehand. But in most
real life dynamic systems, it is not feasible to gather the predefined target outputs.
So, the exact boundaries of the temporal regimes stay hidden. Supervised learning
cannot work in this case. To solve this, we rely on unsupervised learning where
we try to find the hidden structures of the data without referring to any known
or labeled outcomes [16]. Since our multivariate time-series data does not contain
the predefined labels, it is mandatory to use unsupervised learning to discover the
underlying Structural Causal Model (SCM) shifts.
ii. Clustering vs. Temporal Segmentation
One of the primary techniques for unsupervised learning is the method of clustering.
In this method, the data are grouped in a way such that the intra-group similarity
is maximized and the inter-group similarity is minimized. One such well known
clustering technique is k-means clustering. In this method, data points are assigned
to clusters based entirely on their feature proximity, ignoring the sequence of the
data. So, applying standard clustering on time-series data where the sequence matters, destroys the temporal precedence required for causal discovery. To solve this
problem, we can use temporal segmentation. Temporal segmentation safeguards
continuity by grouping contiguous time blocks into discrete clusters. Here, each
cluster represents a temporal regime, and the boundary between two regimes is now
clearly defined, which our causal model may work with [36].
To illustrate this within a real-world context, consider the highly dynamic environmental and tra”c conditions of Dhaka City. A multivariate time-series dataset
tracking hourly parameters, such as tra”c density, weather metrics (humidity, wind
speed), and air quality markers like PM2.5 exhibits distinct, unlabeled temporal
regimes. During the Winter Regime (November-February), cold weather and stagnant air mean tra”c density has a strong, direct causal link to sudden PM2.5 spikes.
However, during the Monsoon Regime (June-August), heavy rainfall washes away
pollutants, breaking or heavily weakening that direct causal link, while the rain itself becomes the primary cause of tra”c gridlock. Standard clustering like k-means
would completely ignore this and may group data based only on features like tra”c
speed, which may cluster winter data and summer data together. Temporal segmentation, however, treats winter and monsoon as continuous, separate blocks of

iii. Requirements for Temporal Segmentation
Although there exist many algorithms which may be used to perform temporal segmentation, some rules or requirements have to be enforced for our specific problem.

2.1.5

Dynamic Causal Models and Time-Series Graphs

In a standard Structural Causal Model (SCM), cause-and-e!ect relationships are
captured only at a single, isolated instant. The underlying mathematics cannot
account for historical dependencies, and a traditional DAG is limited to representing static relations. To evaluate dynamical systems, causal influences from the
past must be explicitly taken into account. Therefore, to properly study structural
breaks across temporal regimes, we must shift from the standard causal framework
introduced in the previous section to Dynamic Causal Models.
i. Unrolling the Graph over Time (Dynamic Bayesian Networks)
A Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) is a directed graphical representation of a
stochastic process [19]. It is important to note that the network structure itself
is not dynamic; rather, it is a fixed topology that models variables across discrete
slices of time. By unrolling these static time slices, the network formally represents
a dynamical system capable of accounting for historical dependencies.
To rigorously define a DBN, three structural requirements must be specified [19]:
1. The Initial State: The structural topology of the first time-slice (t = 0).
2. The Transition Model: The directed connections between historical timeslices (t ↑ ε ) and the current time-slice (t).
3. The Probability Distributions: The specific Conditional Probability Distributions (CPDs) that mathematically govern the relations along these directed connections.

iv. Consistency Throughout Time and Structural Breaks
The mathematical formalizations of Time-Series SCMs and Window Causal Graphs
rely on a fundamental assumption: the underlying causal mechanisms do not change
spontaneously or erratically. It is highly unlikely that a causal direction will abruptly
flip within a stable window; if such instability occurred frequently, the system would
fragment into countless micro-regimes, causing the predictive model to collapse. In
most macroscopic real-world scenarios, a causal link from A to B might weaken
or disappear over time, but an immediate reversal where B suddenly causes A is
highly improbable. This principle of stability is formally known as Consistency
Throughout Time.

According to Assaad et al. [36], a time-series causal graph is consistent throughout

time steps. For instance, if X causes Y with a lag of ε = 1 at time t, it must also
cause Y with the exact same lag at time t + 100.
In the context of this thesis, Consistency Throughout Time serves as the core defining characteristic of a Temporal Regime. As long as the consistency of the causal
graph remains unbroken, that contiguous block of time constitutes a single, independent regime.
However, in real-world dynamical systems, environments evolve and underlying rules
change. When this consistency assumption is violated over time—for example, when
an existing causal edge disappears or a new one emerges—we denote that exact moment as a Structural Break. Consequently, the time steps following this break
transition into an entirely di!erent regime. In observational data, this often manifests as certain variables becoming causally isolated while new cause-and-e!ect relationships are born.

Because the exact temporal boundary (the structural break) between these distinct
causal regimes is hidden within the unlabeled observational data, we cannot rely on
supervised learning to locate it. This dictates the core methodological approach of
this thesis: we must treat the discovery of these structural breaks as an unsupervised learning problem, utilizing clustering algorithms to segment the time-series
based solely on the shifting structures of the underlying Window Causal Graphs.

2.1.6

Algorithmic Framework for Regime Detection and Causal
Discovery

Identifying independent temporal regimes, and more precisely, locating their underlying structural breaks requires a multi-step pipeline. Therefore, our methodology
relies on the integration of multiple algorithms, analytical tools, and optimization
techniques. Within this pipeline, the time-series data is first segmented into distinct

Page 33 of 83 - AI Writing Submission

21

Page 34 of 83 - AI Writing Submission

Submission ID trn:oid:::3:071067466

temporal regimes, and subsequently, the specific causal structure of each isolated
regime is extracted. To achieve this, our framework relies on three core stages:
i. Parameter Estimation and Model Scoring
Ordinary Least Squares (OLS): OLS serves as the baseline optimization
technique in this framework. It mathematically estimates the strength of the
relationship (the edge weight) between a historical cause and a present effect. Because these cause-and-e!ect relationships are temporal, the true edge
weights remain constant within a stable regime but shift when a structural
break occurs. By minimizing the residual sum of squares, OLS evaluates the
baseline error for any given window of time.
Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC): BIC acts as the penalized scoring
metric for this framework. During clustering, if every tiny chunk of time were
segmented independently, the OLS error would drop to near zero, but the
model would severely overfit, rendering the results meaningless. BIC introduces a mathematical penalty for each new regime discovered. Consequently,
it balances the need to minimize OLS error against model complexity, ensuring
the algorithm does not fracture the timeline into an excessive and insignificant
number of segments.
This fundamental trade-o! between minimizing OLS error and minimizing the BIC
penalty ensures the mathematical foundation necessary to deploy search algorithms
to locate the exact structural breaks.
ii. Structural Break Identification
To identify the exact temporal boundaries where structural breaks occur, we utilize
recursive hypothesis testing driven by OLS error evaluation.
Binary Segmentation (BinSeg): Because a time-series may contain multiple hidden regimes, evaluating every single time step independently for a
structural break is computationally intractable. To optimize this, we employ
Binary Segmentation, a recursive ”divide-and-conquer” search strategy. At
each step, the algorithm sweeps the current time window, identifies the single point with the highest Chow Test significance score, and divides the data
at that exact juncture. The recursion then continues independently on those
newly divided sub-segments. Furthermore, because the true number of regimes
is unknown, this method is highly advantageous; it iteratively halves the timeline only when statistically justified, isolating independent regimes without
requiring a predefined regime count.
Chow F-Test: The Chow F-Test serves as the statistical engine that dictates
whether BinSeg should actually execute a split. It evaluates if a structural
break is genuinely happening by comparing the OLS error of a single continuous time window against the combined OLS error of the two segments proposed by BinSeg. If formulating two distinct regression models significantly
reduces the overall residual error, the Chow Test mathematically confirms that
a structural break has occurred and the regime has shifted.

iii. Continuous Causal Graph Discovery
Once the entire time-series is segmented into independent regimes, the final step is
to discover the internal dynamic causal graph governing each regime.
The DYNOTEARS Algorithm: Traditional causal discovery algorithms,
such as the PC algorithm, rely on combinatorial graph search and do not scale
well with dense time-series data. To extract the causal relations and underlying
structures from each independent regime, we utilize DYNOTEARS, a temporal extension of the NOTEARS framework. DYNOTEARS advances DAG
learning by replacing discrete graph search with a continuous, di!erentiable
optimization problem. This formulation allows the algorithm to e”ciently
learn both instantaneous and lagged causal e!ects simultaneously. By applying DYNOTEARS individually to each temporal block isolated by BinSeg, we
can extract the exact localized Window Causal Graph for that specific regime.
iv. Summary and Baseline Assumptions
In summary, this pipeline functions as a comprehensive framework for unsupervised
causal inference: OLS measures the baseline error, BIC prevents model overfitting,
Binary Segmentation recursively divides the timeline, the Chow F-Test statistically
validates the structural breaks, and finally, DYNOTEARS maps the dynamic causal
graphs.
It must be noted that this baseline framework initially operates under the assumption of linear causal relationships. If real-world datasets exhibit strong non-linear
mechanisms, the performance of this specific configuration may degrade. However,
because this pipeline is inherently modular, each algorithm or statistical test can
be substituted with non-linear alternatives optimized for the target dataset. Consequently, this architecture can be adapted to capture the behaviors of highly complex
dynamic systems.

2.2

Causal Discovery and Time Series: Progress
and Key Findings

Over the past two decades, causal discovery has evolved from classical graphical
models to flexible, often deep-learning-based methods that handle mixed data, heterogeneity, and temporal dependence. More recent work focuses specifically on timeseries causal discovery, including nonstationary and high-dimensional settings.

2.2.1

Graphical Causal Discovery

The mathematical formalization of casual discovery has traditionally bifurcated into
constraint-based frameworks, such as PC and Fast Causal Inference (FCI) algorithms, and score-based Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) learning paradigms. While
these classical methodologies established the theoretical bedrock of structure learning, their practical deployment often exposes fundamental vulnerabilities to realworld data pathologies. As reviewed by Glymour et al. [26], issues such as severe
non-linearity, measurement error, nonstationary time series, and latent confounding

the computational bottlenecks of pure score-based searches and the statistical sensitivity of pure constraint-based methods, recent literature has shifted toward hybrid
frameworks. For instance, Kuipers et al. [22] demonstrated that constraint-based
filtering can be used to drastically prune the search space, enabling e”cient Markov
Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling over the remaining DAG space. This integration ultimately facilitates robust Bayesian model averaging even across large-scale

While early structural learning algorithms faced strict data and architectural limitations, recent advances have significantly broadened their scope and reliability.

2.2.2

Heterogeneity, Non-stationarity, and Mechanism Shifts

Modern work exploits distribution shifts across environments as a signal for causal
directions. Huang et al. [29] developed CD-NOD, which learns causal skeletons and
directions from heterogeneous or nonstationary data and estimates low-dimensional
driving forces of changing mechanisms. A related survey highlights non-stationary
time series and heterogeneous multi-set data as central practical challenges [26]. The
Sparse Mechanism Shift hypothesis proposes that only a few conditionals change
across environments; the Mechanism Shift Score (MSS) can then identify the full
causal structure nonparametrically under such sparse shifts [37].
New methods explicitly cluster heterogeneous causal mechanisms: Li et al. [46]
jointly infer latent clusters and their causal graphs from mixed observational data
without environment labels. At the bivariate level, Liu et al. [47] extend hybrid additive noise models to mixtures, enabling causal direction identification and clustering
of mechanism expressions across environments. These developments are directly
relevant to this thesis, which seeks to extend such mechanism clustering to settings
with unknown numbers of regimes and without temporal ordering.

families, and empirically compare them on synthetic and real data. Their conclu-

Temporal Causal Discovery with Unknown Regimes:
CASTOR

The method most closely related to this thesis is CASTOR (Causal Temporal Regime
Structure Learning), introduced by Rahmani and Frossard [48], published at AISTATS 2025. CASTOR is designed to learn causal relationships from multivariate
time series composed of multiple, unknown sequential regimes, concurrently identifying three critical unknowns: the total number of regimes, their sequential boundaries (start and end time points), and the corresponding causal DAG underlying
each regime.
Methodologically, CASTOR employs an Expectation-Maximisation (EM) framework. In the E-step, the algorithm updates posterior probabilities of regime membership based on current causal graphs. In the M-step, it learns each per-regime
DAG by optimising a BIC-penalised score subject to the NOTEARS acyclicity constraint [24]. A key design feature is that CASTOR requires no prior specification
of the number of regimes; it initialises by over-partitioning the time series into
equal-width windows and iteratively merges adjacent segments that exhibit similar
causal structures until convergence. The authors establish theoretical identifiability
for both the ground-truth regimes and their respective DAGs up to a permutation,
assuming Gaussian noise with equal variance.
Experimentally, CASTOR is evaluated on synthetic datasets up to 40 nodes as well
as on two real-world benchmarks: IT infrastructure monitoring data and biosphereatmosphere gas exchange data. Across these tasks, it consistently outperforms CDNOD [29], RPCMCI, and DYNOTEARS [33] in regime detection accuracy (measured by Adjusted Rand Index) and causal graph recovery (measured by SHD and
F1).
CASTOR is the closest existing method to the framework proposed here, and the
comparison is instructive in three respects.
First, both methods target the same problem: joint discovery of an unknown number of sequential regimes and their regime-specific causal graphs, from unlabelled
multivariate time series, without environment labels or a surrogate nonstationarity
index. This shared target distinguishes both from ICP, IRM, and JCI (which require labelled environments) and from CD-NOD and CD-NOTS (which require an
auxiliary index encoding the changing conditions).
Second, the methods di!er fundamentally in how a candidate regime boundary is
evaluated. CASTOR optimises a BIC-penalised likelihood within an expectationmaximisation loop, initialising by over-partitioning into equal-width windows and
merging adjacent segments with similar causal structure. A boundary is therefore
accepted or rejected on the basis of a global score, and the procedure o!ers no explicit
hypothesis test for whether the mechanisms on either side of a proposed boundary
di!er significantly. LIRA instead evaluates each candidate boundary with a formal
statistical test – the Chow F -test on the coe”cient vectors of the structural model
– before any complexity criterion is applied. This yields an interpretable p-value
per boundary and addresses a gap the literature has identified but not closed: the
absence of formal, testable criteria for determining whether two identified regimes
are structurally di!erent rather than artefacts of finite-sample variation.
Third, LIRA’s structural model is not generic. It is derived from the power-law
physics of the Fundamental Plane of Black Hole Activity (Section 2.1.2), which fixes

2.2.6

Continuously Evolving Causal Structure: DyCAST

A complementary frontier result is DyCAST [44], published at ICLR 2025. While
CASTOR models causal structure as piecewise constant across discreete sequential
regimes, DyCAST models it as evolving continuously over time. it achieve this
by using Neural ODEs constrained to remain on the DAG manifold at all times,
successfully capturing smooth causal transitions that discrete regime boundaries
would poorly approximate.
When evaluated across synthetic dynamic datasets, fMRI data, the CausalTime
benchmark, and human motion capture data, DyCAST consistently outperforms all
static baselines and scales substantially beyond methods like DYNOTEARS [33],
NTS-NOTEARS, and TECDI, all of which fail entirely when processing 50 or more
variables. This scalability finding is highly significant because it confirms that even
purpose-built temporal methods struggle at moderate dimensionality. This limitation directly motivates our own scalability analysis in i.i.d. causal clustering (see
Section 2.4.4).
Like CASTOR, DyCAST relies fundamentally on temporal ordering. Its Neural
ODE framework parameterises causal evolution strictly as a function of time, leaving
it with no functional analogue for unordered observations. Consequently, while both
CASTOR and DyCAST provide strong temporal motivation and methodological
inspiration, neither o!ers a direct solution for the i.i.d. setting addressed in this
thesis.

A recent innovation, such as the internal incoherency scores introduced by Faltenbacher
et al. [45] provides a promising path forward by dynamically flagging model misspecifications and finite-sample errors. When these assumptions are violated, performance degrades significantly. Simultaneously, managing high dimensionality and
mixed data types remains as a central algorithmic hurdle. Researchers have approached this challenge from multiple angles, including hybrid Bayesian network

Conclusion of the Survey

Over two decades, causal discovery has progressed from classical DAG learning to
a rich toolbox handling mixed data, heterogeneity, uncertainty, and temporal dynamics. For time series, work spans Granger-based statistics, deep neural methods,
constraint/score-based extensions, and most recently published methods such as

2.3

Foundations of Causal Discovery

Causal discovery refers to inferring causal relationships among variables directly
from observational data, without controlled experiments. The field has its roots in
the constraint-based framework of Spirtes et al. [6], which represents causal structure
as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and recovers it by evaluating statistical conditional independence relationships. Within the paradigm, the PC algorithm begins
with a fully connected graph and removes edges where conditional independence
is detected, producing a Markov equivalence class of DAGs. The FCI algorithm

Page 42 of 83 - AI Writing Submission

30

Submission ID trn:oid:::3:071067466

Page 43 of 83 - AI Writing Submission

Submission ID trn:oid:::3:071067466

extends PC to allow for latent confounders, producing a partial ancestral graph
(PAG).
Score-based methods search over the space of DAGs to find the structure that best
fits the data according to some criterion. The Greedy Equivalence Search (GES)
performs a two-phase greedy search over equivalence classes. Zheng et al. [24]
(NOTEARS) reformulated structure learning as a continuous optimisation problem by expressing acyclicity as a smooth algebraic condition, enabling gradientbased DAG learning at scale. This formulation underpins many derivative methods,
including DYNOTEARS [33], which extends NOTEARS to handle both contemporaneous (intra-slice) and time-lagged (inter-slice) dependencies within a structural
equation model (SEM) framework.
Functional causal models (FCMs) make explicit assumptions about functional form
to achieve full identifiability of causal direction, a resolution that constraint-based
methods cannot achieve from a single observational distribution. The most prominent example, Shimizu et al. [14], shows that if the data-generating process is linear
and noise terms are non-Gaussian, the full causal order is uniquely identifiable via
Independent Component Analysis. Identifiability here means full causal ordering
recovery under the linear non-Gaussian assumption. This result extends to the time
series domain through VAR-LiNGAM [17], which combines the linear non-Gaussian
assumption with vector autoregressive modelling to handle both instantaneous and
lagged causal e!ects.
Despite their contributions, all of these foundational methods share a critical assumption: that the entire dataset originates from a single, fixed causal model. Realworld data frequently violates this homogeneity condition. Empirical data often
arise from multiple distinct latent processes, such as varying experimental conditions, heterogeneous population subgroups, shifting temporal intervals, or di!ering
biological states, where each group is governed by its own unique causal structure.
When such mixed-mechanism data are processed by a traditional single-environment
algorithm, the optimization routinely collapses into a spurious, averaged graph that
fails to accurately represent any of the true underlying systems. This critical limitation highlights the need for heterogeneous structure learning and provides the
primary motivation for the research directions evaluated in the following sections.

2.3.1

Causal Discovery from Nonstationary and Heterogeneous Data

A substantial body of research extends causal discovery to settings where the datagenerating process changes across domains, time periods, or experimental conditions,
exploiting the insights that such nonstationarity and heterogeneity carry structural
information can improve causal identification.
The foundational contribution is Invariant Causal Prediction (ICP) [20], which identifies the direct causal parents of a target variable as the minimal predictor set
whose conditional relationship with the target remains invariant across known environments. This elegantly exploits the stability of causal mechanisms, but requires
environment labels to be provided in advance.
Huang et al. [29] developed CD-NOD, which exploits distribution shifts across domains or over time to improve causal skeleton recovery and edge orientation. A
key insight is that data heterogeneity benefits causal identification: shifting mecha-

CD-NOD requires an auxiliary variable (domain index or time stamp) to model
nonstationarity, but not hard environment labels.
Sadeghi et al. [49] introduced CD-NOTS as a direct extension of CD-NOD for time
series, correcting its treatment of lagged relationships and providing principled guidance for conditional independence test selection (see Section 2.2 for details). Perry
et al. [37] advanced the theory through the Sparse Mechanism Shift hypothesis: if
only a small number of causal mechanisms change between environments, the full
causal graph becomes identifiable with high probability. But it again requires known
environment membership.
In the temporal domain, Cheng et al. [44] (ICLR 2025) models the continuous temporal evolution of the causal DAG using Neural ODEs (see Section 2.2 for the detailed
comparison with CASTOR). Its strong scalability and performance results deliver
a clear lesson: real-world causal structures are not static, and methods that model
this dynamic nature recover substantially more accurate and interpretable causal
graphs.
Arjovsky et al. [25] introduced Invariant Risk Minimisation (IRM) as a learningtheoretic complement to ICP, framing multi-environment causal learning as an outof-distribution generalisation problem. IRM encourages reliance on causally stable
features, but requires known environment partitions and its guarantees weaken when
the number of environments is small relative to the number of features.
Taken together, these works establish a clear principle: heterogeneity and nonstationarity are assets, not obstacles, for causal learning. However, they all require
either environment labels, an auxiliary nonstationarity index (CD-NOD [29], CDNOTS [49]), or temporal ordering (CASTOR [48], DyCAST [44]). Recovering regime
structure from purely i.i.d. data without any such external information is the central
gap this thesis targets.

2.3.2

Mixture Models and Causal Clustering

A smaller but rapidly growing body of work addresses simultaneous clustering and
causal structure learning, targeting scenarios where the environment partition must
itself be inferred. These works recognise that clustering and causal discovery must be
solved jointly: sequential approaches su!er from error propagation, since incorrect
cluster assignments corrupt causal structure learning and vice versa.
An early and influential contribution is the Specific and Shared Causal Model
(SSCM) framework of Huang et al. [27], which models data as arising from a mixture
of causal mechanisms and clusters observations by the mechanism that generated
them, distinguishing regime-specific from shared causal relations. However, SSCM
requires the number of regimes to be specified in advance.
Chen et al. [39] proposed CCSL (Causal Clustering Structure Learning), published
in IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, and the most directly relevant prior work to this thesis. CCSL addresses learning causal structure
from non-i.i.d. data collected from multiple unknown environments, where neither
the number of environments nor their assignment is known. The key innovation
is a Causality-related Chinese Restaurant Process (Causal CRP), a Bayesian nonparametric clustering procedure assigning observations to clusters based on causal
structure similarity rather than feature-space distance. A variational inference ap-

2.3.3

Joint Causal Inference from Multiple Contexts

Mooij et al. [31] developed Joint Causal Inference (JCI), a unifying graphical framework for causal discovery from multiple observational and interventional contexts.
JCI encodes context as additional nodes in the causal graph, allowing both causal
structure and intervention type to be inferred jointly. The approach is theoretically
general and subsumes many multi-environment special cases, but presupposes that
the contexts themselves are known and pre-specified, placing it in the same category
as other labelled-environment methods.
The broader causal representation learning literature has explored disentangling
latent causal variables from high-dimensional observations via variational autoencoders or other deep generative models. These methods share the goal of uncovering
hidden causal structure, but typically focus on identifying latent variables rather
than partitioning observations by causal regime, and are evaluated predominantly
in image or video domains rather than tabular scientific data.

2.4

Summary of Key Findings

The literature reviewed reveals a clear progressive trajectory. The discipline has advanced from single-environment causal discovery, through multi-environment methods that exploit known partitions or auxiliary nonstationarity indices, to joint inference of causal structure and data clustering without prior environment information.
This section consolidates key methodological trends, consistent findings, contradictions and limitations, and articulates the specific research gaps addressed by this
thesis alongside an explicit statement of scope.

2.4.1

Methodological Trends

Within foundational causal discovery, constraint-based methods (PC, FCI) and
score-based methods (GES, NOTEARS [24], DYNOTEARS [33]) represent the two
primary paradigms, with FCMs (LiNGAM [14], VAR-LiNGAM [17], ANMs) occupying an assumption-heavy but important niche. As surveyed by Assaad et al. [36],
these families di!er in their core assumptions: Granger causality relies on predictive
precedence; constraint-based methods exploit conditional independence; noise-based
methods leverage distributional assumptions (e.g. non-Gaussianity); and score-based

methods optimise a global criterion such as BIC. Neural extensions (TCDF [28],
NTiCD [38], CUTS+ [40], [50]) demonstrate that neural architectures can learn
causal structure from high-dimensional or non-stationary time series. As noted in
Section 2.2.4, these methods are inherently sequential and inapplicable to the i.i.d.

In multi-environment and nonstationary research, the use of invariance or distributional shift as a guiding principle is the defining methodological commitment. This

study [49] further demonstrates the practical cost of assumption misspecification.

accurate results. CASTOR’s EM-based joint learning establishes that alternating
between soft regime assignment and causal graph estimation is theoretically principled and empirically superior.
In the causal clustering literature, Bayesian nonparametric methods, especially Chinese Restaurant Process-based approaches are the dominant strategy for jointly inferring cluster assignments and causal structures [27, 39]. CCSL [39] demonstrates

ideas to mixed-type data without temporal ordering. Temporal extensions such as
MCD [42] and CASTOR [48] adapt these principles via EM or variational inference. At the bivariate level, Liu et al. [47] provide a complementary perspective on
mechanism-aware clustering.

2.4.2

Consistent Findings

Several consistent findings emerge across the reviewed works.
1. Causal structure varies substantially across regimes; methods ignoring this
heterogeneity produce spurious averaged graphs that accurately represent none
of the true underlying mechanisms [29, 39, 42, 46, 48].
2. Joint inference of cluster assignments and causal structures consistently outperforms sequential pipelines [27, 39, 46, 48].
3. Causal structure similarity is a more stable clustering signal than feature-space
distance or correlation, as demonstrated by the CCSL experiments [39], where
causally-aware clustering substantially outperforms k-means, DBSCAN, and
OPTICS across all tested conditions.
4. Real-world data consistently violates the stationarity assumption, as shown by
CD-NOTS [49] across financial case studies and by CASTOR [48] in biosphereatmosphere and IT monitoring data.
5. No single causal discovery method is universally best; performance depends
critically on whether the method’s assumptions match the data-generating
process [36].

Contradictions and Limitations

Invariance-based multi-environment methods (ICP [20], IRM [25]) require known
environment partitions. When environment labels are missing or incorrect, their
theoretical guarantees collapse, and no consensus exists on how to robustly recover
environment structure in a purely unsupervised manner.
In the temporal domain, a tension exists between methods designed for stationary data and those for nonstationary data. CD-NOTS [49] shows that applying stationarity-assuming algorithms to real-world financial data produces spurious
causal graphs. Yet many causal clustering methods, including CCSL [39], assume
stationarity within each cluster. While CASTOR [48] handles sequential nonstationarity, it assumes piecewise-constant regimes and does not address i.i.d. clustering without temporal order. Its identifiability guarantees further require Gaussian
equal-variance noise, which may be overly restrictive.
In the causal clustering literature, the core tension is between model expressivity
and identifiability. CCSL [39] provides rigorous identifiability under the linear nonGaussian assumption (full causal ordering recovery, as in LiNGAM [14]), but this
may be too restrictive for nonlinear data. More expressive frameworks such as
HCL [46] and MCD [42] relax these assumptions but sacrifice formal identifiability
guarantees. For instance, the HCL claims identifiability under “mild conditions”
not fully specified in the current preprint. CASTOR [48] provides identifiability for
temporal data under Gaussian equal-variance assumptions. Liu et al. [47] o!er a
potential bivariate middle ground, but the appropriate trade-o! between expressivity
and identifiability for unsupervised causal clustering remains open.
Evaluation methodology is also inconsistent across works: some papers measure
clustering quality independently of causal graph recovery; others report only graph
accuracy. The absence of a unified benchmark makes cross-method comparison
di”cult. Internal incoherency scores [45] o!er a promising direction for assumptionviolation detection, but have not yet been adapted to the causal clustering setting.

2.4.4

Research Gaps

The reviewed literature reveals several notable gaps. For each gap, the scope with
respect to this thesis is stated explicitly.
Absence of a formal invariance test in temporal regime discovery. Methods requiring labelled environments (ICP, IRM, JCI) or an auxiliary nonstationarity index (CD-NOD, CD-NOTS) are inapplicable where the latent state is itself
unknown. Among methods that discover regimes jointly with causal structure
from unlabelled series, CASTOR and DyCAST represent the state of the art, but
neither subjects a proposed regime boundary to an explicit statistical hypothesis test; boundaries are accepted on the basis of a global optimisation score.
The consequence is that a discovered boundary carries no associated significance
statement. This thesis addresses that gap directly by constructing a boundary
criterion from the Chow test for equality of regression coe”cients, extended to
accommodate temporal autocorrelation, and by pairing it with an independent
complexity criterion so that statistical significance and model parsimony are evaluated as separate conditions.

Risk Management

The biggest risk in this thesis is that real astrophysical time-series data from black
hole accretion disks, like X-ray light curves, often have irregular patterns, noise, and
limited quantity. This makes it hard to reliably discover causal graphs and separate
unknown regimes.
Unlike controlled lab experiments, it is impossible to label which accretion state
produced each data point, and the actual number of distinct causal mechanisms in
the data is unknown. Also, hidden or unobserved variables, such as changes in the
magnetic field, may act as confounders, creating false causal connections between
the measured features. Many standard causal discovery algorithms assume there
are no cycles or feedback loops, but accretion disks show strong feedback between
variables like temperature, density, and accretion rate.
To manage these risks, the research will rely primarily on simulated data where the
true causal graphs are known. It will also use algorithms designed for time-series
data that handle time delays and hidden variables. The results will be compared
across di!erent methods to ensure stability against random noise and small sample

Stage 3: causal e!ect estimation and validation

Stages 1 and 2 yield a graph; Stage 3 quantifies the e!ects it encodes and assesses
how far those quantities should be trusted.
For each discovered parent of FR within each regime, the causal e!ect is estimated
by backdoor adjustment, conditioning on the remaining parents. Unpenalised regression is used here, for the reason given in Section 4.3.4: with the structure already
determined, regularisation contributes only bias. The lag term log FR (t ↑ 1) must be
included in every adjustment set, since the predictors are autocorrelated with their
own past and FR (t ↑ 1) lies downstream of that past; omitting it allows a predictor
to absorb part of the lag term’s e!ect.
Three refutation procedures are applied. A random common cause test injects
an irrelevant synthetic covariate, under which a valid estimate should be unchanged.
A placebo treatment test replaces the treatment with a permuted version, under
which the estimated e!ect should collapse to zero; this is the diagnostic that would
expose a procedure generating e!ects from noise. A data subset test re-estimates
on an 80
Sensitivity analysis is then applied in the partial-R2 parameterisation of Cinelli
and Hazlett, which reports a robustness value: the minimum proportion of residual
variance in both treatment and outcome that an unobserved confounder would need
to explain in order to reduce the estimate to zero. Unobserved confounding cannot be
excluded from observational data, and Section 1.6 identifies it as a principal challenge
for this application; the robustness value converts that acknowledged limitation into
a stated numerical threshold against which domain knowledge can be brought to
bear.
Finally, graph falsification tests whether the conditional-independence implications of the entire recovered DAG are consistent with the data, by comparing its
violation count against randomly permuted graphs. Where the preceding tests validate individual e!ects conditional on the graph, this interrogates the graph itself.

Chapter 6
Conclusion
This thesis has introduced LIRA (Latent Invariant Regime Analysis), a self-supervised
causal framework for discovering latent causal regimes and their regime-specific
causal structures directly from unlabelled black hole accretion time series. Motivated by the power-law physics of the Fundamental Plane of Black Hole Activity,
the framework models accretion observables within a log-linear structural causal
specification in which black hole mass is absorbed into the intercept and temporal
state variation is carried by four spectral-state diagnostics. Regime boundaries are
located by greedy recursive partitioning driven by a first-order autoregressive Chow
F -test, and are subject to an independent complexity criterion derived from the
Gaussian likelihood, which determines the number of regimes automatically. Within
each discovered regime, DYNOTEARS recovers contemporaneous and lagged causal
structure, and the resulting e!ects are subjected to refutation testing, sensitivity
analysis, and graph falsification.
Validation on synthetic accretion data constructed so that the regimes are distinguishable by causal mechanism alone establishes that the framework recovers the
true boundary to within four observations of ten thousand, determines the correct
number of regimes without prior specification, and recovers every structural coe”cient to within 0.017 of its generating value, including the correct identification of
the causally isolated variable in each state. Structure-blind clustering baselines fail
almost entirely on the same data, and a pooled model fitted without segmentation
recovers a structure corresponding to neither regime – averaging a coe”cient that
is strongly negative in one state and positive in the other almost to zero, and inflating the persistence parameter by more than half. The two-gate design is shown to
be load-bearing rather than redundant: at both terminal nodes the invariance test
alone would have partitioned further, and only the complexity criterion prevents it.
Three limitations are acknowledged. Empirical validation to date rests on synthetic
rather than observational data; the linearity and piecewise-stationarity assumptions
approximate what are in reality nonlinear and continuously evolving accretion dynamics; and the identifiability argument advanced in Section 4.6 holds for the collider specification adopted here and would not extend without modification to a
model containing causal chains among the spectral variables. The sensitivity analysis reported in Section 5.6.2 addresses the hidden-confounding concern raised at the
outset not by excluding it, which observational data cannot do, but by quantifying
how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be – and in doing so establishes that the soft state’s weaker coupling warrants correspondingly weaker causal

Page 78 of 83 - AI Writing Submission

66

Submission ID trn:oid:::3:071067466

Page 79 of 83 - AI Writing Submission

Submission ID trn:oid:::3:071067466

claims than the hard state’s.
The immediate direction for future work is application to real observational data
from the WATCHDOG catalogue and to well-monitored sources such as GRS 1915+105
and Cygnus X-1, where the discovered regimes may be compared against expert
spectral-state classifications. Beyond this, extension to nonlinear intra-regime mechanisms, comparison against CASTOR on a common benchmark, and relaxation of
the piecewise-constant assumption in the direction of continuously evolving structure represent the natural continuations of this work.
